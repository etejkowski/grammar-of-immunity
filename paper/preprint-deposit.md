## The route

Three moves, in this order. The first two can both happen today.

| # | Action | Gatekeeper | Yields |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Zenodo–GitHub release of the repository | none | code + data DOI, today |
| 2 | OSF Preprints deposit of the manuscript | moderated, 1–3 days | preprint DOI, discoverable |
| 3 | Ronin Institute or IGDORE affiliation | application | unlocks bioRxiv later |

Then the four inquiry emails go out with whichever DOI exists.

### Why this order, and one risk worth knowing before you start

OSF Preprints is the best preprint home available without an affiliation: run by
the non-profit Center for Open Science, indexed, DOI-issuing, no institutional
requirement. It is my first choice for the paper.

But it is **pre-moderated**, and its moderation policy states: *"Content
completely or mostly generated by large language model (LLM) tools or other
artificial intelligence (AI) tools is not appropriate for OSF Preprints."* Your
manuscript carries a prominent, accurate declaration that an AI assistant drafted
and revised text and implemented the analysis code. A moderator could read that as
falling under the policy.

I am not suggesting you soften the declaration. It is true, it is what Elsevier and
Springer ask for, and removing it to get past a filter would be the wrong trade.
What follows from it is only this: **do not let the inquiry emails wait on OSF.**
Do the Zenodo release first, because Zenodo has no moderation of this kind and
therefore no way to stall you.

If OSF declines or has not moved in five days, deposit the manuscript PDF to
Zenodo as a second record (upload type *Publication → Preprint*) and use that DOI.
Preprints.org (MDPI, free, Crossref DOI, institutional email "recommended" not
required) is the other fallback; its AI policy is disclosure-based rather than
prohibitive. Post the paper to **one** preprint server only — the code archive is a
separate artifact and does not count as duplication.

## 1. Zenodo–GitHub release (30 minutes, no gatekeeper)

This gives the code and data their own DOI. It is standard practice, strengthens
the reproducibility claim, and is not duplicate publication.

Two metadata files are now in the repository root and must be **committed and
pushed before you cut the release** — Zenodo reads them out of the release tarball
at the moment it is created:

- `.zenodo.json` — title, both authors with ORCIDs, description, keywords, licence
- `CITATION.cff` — makes GitHub show a "Cite this repository" button

Steps:

1. Sign in at https://zenodo.org — use "Log in with GitHub" or with ORCID.
2. Go to https://zenodo.org/account/settings/github and flip the switch for
   `etejkowski/grammar-of-immunity`. The repository is public, which the
   integration requires.
3. Confirm the metadata is on GitHub. `.zenodo.json`, `CITATION.cff` and `LICENSE`
   are committed and pushed; Zenodo reads them out of the release tarball, so if
   you ever change them, push before cutting a release:
   ```bash
   git push origin main
   ```
4. On GitHub, **Releases → Draft a new release**. Tag `v1.0.0`, title
   "Grammar of Immunity — manuscript submission version". For the notes, one
   paragraph is enough: what the repository contains and that it accompanies the
   manuscript.
5. Publish the release. Zenodo picks it up within a few minutes and mints **two**
   DOIs: a *concept* DOI that always resolves to the newest version, and a
   *version* DOI for `v1.0.0`. **Cite the concept DOI.**
6. Afterwards, add the DOI badge to `README.md` and the DOI to the manuscript's
   "Data and code availability" section, which currently names commit `53226f2`
   only. Paste the DOI to the assistant and both happen in one pass.

Licensing is settled: `LICENSE` releases the analysis code under MIT and states
that the manuscript text and figures under `paper/` are CC-BY 4.0, which is the
same licence the preprint deposit uses. `.zenodo.json` declares `mit` for the
software record. Third-party inputs — VDJdb, IMMREP23, NetTCR-2.2 — are fetched
rather than redistributed and keep their own terms.

## 3. Affiliation, for the next paper (and to unlock bioRxiv)

Two organizations exist specifically for researchers in your position, and either
satisfies what bioRxiv is asking for:

- **Ronin Institute for Independent Scholarship** — a US non-profit whose entire
  purpose is affiliation and community for scholars outside universities. No
  institutional sponsor needed; there is a vetting process.
- **IGDORE** (Institute for Globally Distributed Open Research and Education) —
  the same idea, internationally distributed.

With either, bioRxiv becomes available for a resubmission of this paper or the
next one. The university route — affiliate or visiting-scholar status at SIUE, SLU
or WashU — carries more weight but needs a faculty sponsor and takes months.
Adding an academic co-author solves the affiliation problem and helps in peer
review, and is worth considering on its own merits.

Do not list a K-12 employer. bioRxiv's stated reason is oversight of research
activities, which a school cannot provide for an immunology paper, and naming them
invites precisely the dispute the policy exists to adjudicate.
